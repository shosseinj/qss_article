\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Event-driven classifiers process not only where an event occurs but also when it occurs. Retiming can therefore change a prediction while preserving event identities, spatial coordinates, polarity and total count. Surrogate-gradient learning makes spiking dynamics trainable \cite{neftci2019}, and recent spike-retiming attacks demonstrate that timing is itself an adversarial input dimension \cite{yu2026}. Understanding this dimension requires specifying the representation, the admissible temporal changes and the computation available to an attacker.

Hybrid quantum spiking classifiers introduce an additional transformation between event timing and class logits. A classical spiking frontend summarizes the stream, a learned angle map drives a variational circuit, and quantum measurement probabilities become classifier features. This raises two distinct empirical questions: can timing-only changes disrupt the implemented hybrid classifier, and do those failures establish a specifically quantum mechanism? An attack on the classifier answers the first question; interpreting its mechanism requires additional representation-level evidence.

This paper studies an implemented eight-qubit model, QSNN-v3, alongside separate classical controls. Its central finding is that \textbf{retiming outcomes must be interpreted within their specified input representation and computational budget}. Within the timestamp experiment, the numerical comparison changes with the definition of matched computation; across the separate timestamp and packet-grid studies, differences in victims, populations and budget units preclude a causal representation ablation or a pooled robustness ranking. Native-event timestamp attacks cause 3--5 failures among 100 jointly clean-correct hybrid validation examples, with only zero to two additional failures for TEMP-DRIFT-v2 over timestamp PGD. A separate controlled packet-grid study produces 98.0--100\% attack success against the custom SNN but 0--1.9\% against three reference architectures.

The contribution is therefore an evaluation of an implemented hybrid pipeline under explicit temporal constraints, rather than a claim that quantum processing is intrinsically vulnerable or robust. Recent quantum-classifier studies examine how encodings, model structure and adversarial access affect robustness \cite{wendlinger2024comparative,gong2024randomized,dowling2026guarantees}. Our setting adds the event-to-feature transformation: the adversary changes event timing before a classical spiking frontend and a quantum probability readout. This distinction makes end-to-end feasibility and computational accounting necessary parts of the experimental claim.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A fully specified hybrid evaluation target.} We document the Conv/LIF frontend, learned temporal-to-angle mapping, eight-qubit data re-uploading circuit and sole probability readout, with a margin-based interpretation and five-seed validation conditional on a shared pretrained frontend.
\item \textbf{A paired comparison under three computational controls.} We evaluate timestamp PGD and derivative-free TEMP-DRIFT-v2 under equal candidate count, equal forward-pass count and calibrated elapsed time, reporting exact success counts and paired tests on the same examples.
\item \textbf{A controlled classical retiming comparison.} We quantify the large attack-outcome spread across four architectures under binary and integer packet representations, while explicitly separating clean accuracy, attack success, evaluation population and budget units.
\end{enumerate}
The resulting contribution is an empirical account of timing-only vulnerability in the implemented systems.
